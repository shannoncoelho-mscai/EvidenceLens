THE CHALLENGE

Why is scientific evidence difficult to understand?

Research can improve our understanding, but individual studies may have limitations or disagree with one another. Headlines and social media posts can oversimplify what researchers found.

01

Too much information

Thousands of research findings and online claims compete for our attention. Finding relevant, reliable evidence can be difficult.

02

Conflicting results

Studies may reach different conclusions because they use different methods, participants, measurements, or conditions.

03

Misleading claims

A headline may exaggerate a finding or suggest that an association proves cause and effect.

LEARN THE BASICS

Understanding different types of evidence

Different research methods answer different questions. The strength of evidence depends on the research question, study design, quality, and possible sources of bias.

01

Individual research study

Researchers investigate a question using observations, experiments, surveys, or other methods. One study can provide useful evidence, but it may not settle the question.

02

Observational study

Researchers observe what happens without assigning participants to an intervention. These studies can reveal patterns and associations, but confounding factors may affect the results.

03

Randomised controlled trial

Participants are randomly assigned to different groups. When well designed and appropriate, this can help researchers assess whether an intervention causes an effect.

04

Systematic review

Researchers use a planned method to find, select, and critically assess relevant studies addressing a research question.

05

Meta-analysis

Researchers use statistical methods to combine results from sufficiently comparable studies. The reliability of the conclusion still depends on the underlying evidence.

Remember: No single study type is automatically best for every question. Evaluate the research methods, quality, relevance, and limitations.

LOOK AT THE WHOLE PICTURE

Counting studies is not the same as reviewing evidence

Simply counting how many studies support an idea can hide important differences in study quality, size, and methods.

Simple vote counting

"How many studies say yes or no?"

  • Treats study results like votes.
  • May ignore study size and quality.
  • May overlook the magnitude of effects.
  • Can give a misleading overall impression.

Systematic review

"What does the relevant evidence collectively show?"

  • Uses a planned search strategy.
  • Applies stated study-selection criteria.
  • Assesses study quality and limitations.
  • Explains uncertainty and conflicting findings.

A systematic review can also reach uncertain conclusions when the available studies are weak, inconsistent, or insufficient.

SCIENCE CHANGES WITH EVIDENCE

When scientific advice changes

Scientific understanding can change when new evidence becomes available or when researchers reassess earlier findings. A change in advice should be understood in its historical context.

CASE STUDY 01

Infant sleep recommendations

Advice about infant sleep has changed as researchers gathered more evidence about sleep position and the risk of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS).

This illustrates why public-health advice should be reviewed as evidence develops, rather than relying indefinitely on older assumptions.

For real-world decisions, follow current guidance from recognised public-health authorities.

CASE STUDY 02

One finding versus the wider evidence

An individual study may attract attention because its result is surprising. Later research may support, qualify, or fail to reproduce that result.

Looking at the methods and the wider body of evidence helps prevent one headline from being mistaken for a final scientific conclusion.

THINK BEFORE YOU TRUST

Better questions lead to better understanding.

Scientific literacy is not about believing every study or rejecting every claim. It is about examining methods, checking sources, understanding uncertainty, and updating our conclusions when the evidence changes.
